## About the project

Futura Kids is the extension of Editora Futura’s English platform for the early years, for children who can’t read yet. An interface based on text, points and rankings doesn’t work for them, and the main platform was designed for students who already read. The proposal is a kids version, switched on by the student’s age and guided by Mr. Brain’s voice.

## Challenges

- Make the platform work for children who can’t read yet, from textual to sensory.
- Adapt the four-skill activities to audio, images and touch.
- Guide navigation with Mr. Brain’s voice, in short sentences for children.
- Show progress without points or rankings.

## Business context

Futura Kids brings Editora Futura’s English platform to the early years. The extension broadens the publisher’s coverage to a school stage the main platform didn’t serve, without creating a separate product: the idea is that the kids version is switched on by the student’s age, inside the same platform.

Futura Kids could be understood as Editora Futura’s front dedicated to the world of children, bringing together learning experiences, educational content and digital resources designed especially for kids.

In that context, it would work as a space where learning goes beyond consuming content: the child explores, plays, experiments, takes on challenges and builds skills while learning.

### What would Futura Kids’ role be?

The proposal could be organized into three pillars:

- **Learning by exploring:** themed worlds, Mr. Brain’s Cinema and activities that swap text for audio, images and touch.
- **Playing with a purpose:** tongue twisters, karaoke, 1-minute challenges and a sticker album that fills up with every activity completed.
- **Growing without fear of mistakes:** always-positive feedback, progress without points or rankings, and mistakes treated as an invitation to try again.

## UX process

The same Double Diamond as the main platform, with a different focus: here, children who can’t read yet. Design Thinking guided empathy with the child, Jobs to Be Done framed what they need to be able to do, and Lean UX treated each choice as a bet to revisit.

- **Discover:** research on school-age children, organized in three areas: what sustains engagement, what they need and what gets in the way.
- **Define:** the child’s job, understanding what to do and moving on without depending on reading, became the guideline “from textual to sensory” and the rule “the child never fails, they just try again”.
- **Develop:** activities for the four skills adapted to audio, images and touch, navigation through Mr. Brain’s voice, themed worlds and progression. The first exploration, with XP, badges and level up, was revisited and replaced by the sticker album.
- **Deliver:** the April 2026 product guideline and screen concepts, with rules every screen must follow and next steps (a mode switched on by age, and onboarding).

## Hypothesis

The product bet: replacing text with audio, image and touch lets children use the platform and feel capable without depending on reading. The rule behind every screen was that the child never fails, they just try again.

## Research

The research organized school-age children’s needs along three axes:

- **What keeps them engaged:** games, stories, music, challenges and exploration.
- **What they need:** clarity, encouragement, variety and progress.
- **What gets in the way:** repetition, long instructions and fear of making mistakes.

Each axis became a design decision: visual instructions and explicit goals to reduce reliance on reading, celebrating wins to encourage, rotating activities against monotony and visible milestones to make progress tangible.

![Insight map: what the child likes, what they need and the difficulties they face, with Mr. Brain at the center](img:k8)

## Decisions and trade-offs

- **From text-based to sensory.** Listening went from “listen and type” to “listen and tap the right picture”; Reading became visual interaction; Writing became voice and tracing letters with a finger; text instructions became audio, always.
- **Stickers instead of points.** The first exploration used XP, badges and level-ups. It was dropped because young children don’t understand numeric scores: each completed activity earns a sticker that fills the unit’s scene, and the student sees no levels or points.
- **No ranking.** The main platform’s ranking became individual visual progression.
- **100% positive feedback.** In speaking activities, AI assesses rhythm and intonation in the background, but what the child hears is always motivational, never technical.
- **Messages between classmates only in a moderated space.** Mr. Brain’s Mail exchanges voice messages and stickers.

![First progression exploration with XP, badge, level-up and a new world, later replaced by the sticker album](img:k6)

## Solution

### Voice navigation

Narration is on by default. Every screen starts with an audio instruction, every block has a replay button and Mr. Brain shows visually when he is speaking. Because children process audio more slowly, the child controls the speed (0.5x, 0.75x or 1x). Mr. Brain speaks in short sentences and takes on five roles (lines translated from Portuguese):

- **Guide:** “Here’s your mission!”
- **Coach:** “Almost! Try again.”
- **Cheerleader:** “You did it!”
- **Host:** “Ready?”
- **Companion:** “Let’s explore!”

### Worlds, missions and activities

Learning happens in themed worlds (school, home, park, airport). Each unit opens with Mr. Brain’s Cinema, a short video that introduces vocabulary without asking for any action. Activities became games: tongue twisters, kids’ karaoke, sound differences (“ship or sheep?”, with two pictures and zero text), a 1-minute challenge, Mr. Brain’s Mail and pre-reading with rhymes and letter tracing.

The city map organizes the worlds into everyday places, and each place opens a set of activities.

On the board, the child moves square by square through the four skills and finds rewards along the way.

### Progress

The platform’s badges become stickers (Social Butterfly becomes a butterfly; Active Voice, a microphone). Correct answers get a sound reward, and mistakes become an invitation: “Almost! Shall we try again?”

## Validation

The guideline sets rules every screen must meet: start with an audio instruction, have a replay button, show when Mr. Brain is speaking, show no negative terms or technical errors, and show no levels or points.

## Post-launch results

As next steps, the guideline recommends an early-years mode switched on automatically by the student’s age and an onboarding that teaches the child to listen, tap and speak.

## Learnings

- Points, rankings and text, which work for older students, become barriers for children who can’t read yet. The first exploration with XP and badges made that clear, and the sticker album took its place.
